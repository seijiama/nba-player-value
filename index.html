    How the models work

    Market value

    0.71 test R²

    A random forest trained on per-game box-score stats, games played, minutes and age. It predicts salary as a share of that season's cap. Because it knows age, it learns how the league actually pays: cheap rookie-scale deals for young players and bigger maximums for veterans.a

    Production value

    0.57 test R²

    The same model with age removed. It can only price what a player does on the court, so it explains less of real salaries but sits closer to pure on-court worth.a The gap between the two predictions is the CBA discount shown on each player card.

    Data
    Per-game stats and salaries from Basketball-Reference, 2015–16 through 2025–26.
    Who's included
    Players with at least 55 games in a season, so injury-shortened seasons don't skew pay comparisons.
    Residual
    (Actual salary − predicted salary) ÷ that season's salary cap. Positive means overpaid relative to the model. Surplus is the same gap with the sign flipped, in dollars.
    Why % of cap
    The cap more than doubled, from $70.0M in 2015–16 to $154.6M in 2025–26, so the same dollar figure means very different things across seasons.
    Train / test
    80% of 2015–16 to 2024–25 player-seasons trained the models; the other 20% were held out to measure the R² above. 2025–26 is a pure projection with no salaries yet.b
    1. a The market model reflects how the NBA actually pays, including the rookie scale and maximum-salary tiers set by years of service. The production model is age-blind and closer to pure on-court worth. ↩
    2. b The 2015–16 to 2024–25 seasons are in-sample: the models trained on that data, so historical residuals look slightly better than the models would do on new seasons. Rows marked “held-out” on a player card are the exception. ↩
